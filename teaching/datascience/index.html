Part 1 · Why inference is needed
01 · Introduction

What Is Statistical Inference?

Imagine you're deciding whether to bring an umbrella to class. You look outside—dark clouds, windy—and you think, "It's probably going to rain." You just made an inference. You took limited information (what you can see right now) and drew a conclusion about what's likely to happen.

Statistical inference works the same way, but with data instead of weather. Statistical inference is the process of using data from a sample to draw conclusions about a larger population—while being honest about how uncertain those conclusions are.

💡 Think in Stories, Not Formulas

Population: Not "all values in set N" → Think: "Every student at PCCC"

Sample: Not "subset of N" → Think: "50 students from your CIS class"

Why? We study the sample to learn about the population—because we usually can't survey everyone. And because a sample is only part of the population, our conclusions always carry some uncertainty.

Real-World Example 1: The Umbrella Decision

Step 1: Observe

Limited Information

You look outside and see dark clouds. That's your "sample" of information.

Step 2: Infer

Draw Conclusion

Based on past experience (dark clouds → rain), you conclude it'll probably rain. That's inference—a conclusion that is likely, not guaranteed.

Real-World Example 2: Restaurant Food Quality

Population

All Meals at Restaurant

Every dish the restaurant has ever served and will serve.

Sample

Your 3 Visits

You ate there 3 times, food was great each time. You infer: "This restaurant is good!" With only 3 visits, though, you should be less confident than if you'd eaten there 30 times.

✓ Key Takeaway

We use small pieces of information (samples) to make educated guesses about the bigger picture (population). That's the heart of statistical inference. The rest of this lesson is about collecting that information well, testing claims with it, and avoiding common mistakes.

Inference is one step inside a bigger process. The scientific method shows where it fits:

1

Ask a Question

What do you want to know? (e.g., "Do PCCC students get enough sleep?")

2

Background Research

Has anyone studied this before? What did they find?

3

Make a testable, educated guess about the answer.

4

Gather information through surveys, experiments, or observations. How you collect it decides what you can conclude (Part 2).

5

Analyze the Data

Summarize, visualize, and run the appropriate statistical test (Parts 3 and 5).

6

Draw Conclusions

Do the data support your hypothesis? How confident can you be, and what could have gone wrong (Part 4)?

Part 2 · How evidence is collected
03 · Study Design

Observational Studies vs. Experiments

Before running any test, ask: how were these data collected? The answer limits which conclusions are justified.

Observational Study Randomized Experiment
You observe and record what naturally happens without interference. You actively assign treatments, using random assignment to decide who gets which.
Example: Survey students about their sleep and GPA. Example: Randomly assign students to a 6-hour or 8-hour sleep schedule, then measure test scores.
Generally supports: Associations (things move together) Can support: Causal conclusions—when the experiment is well designed and carried out
⚠️ Correlation ≠ Causation

Just because two things are related doesn't mean one causes the other. In observational data, a hidden factor called a confounding variable can create the relationship.

What's a Confounding Variable?

Think of it like this: It's a hidden "third thing" that affects both variables, making them look connected even when one isn't causing the other.

Example 1: Umbrellas and Getting Wet

Observation

Umbrellas → Wet People

You notice people carrying umbrellas are more likely to get wet. Does the umbrella cause wetness?

Confounding Variable

Rain!

People carry umbrellas when it's raining. Rain causes both umbrella-carrying AND getting wet. The umbrella doesn't cause wetness!

Example 2: Ice Cream and Drowning

Observation

Ice Cream Sales ↔ Drowning Deaths

Months with high ice cream sales also tend to have more drownings. Does ice cream cause drowning?

Confounding Variable

Summer Heat!

Hot weather increases ice cream sales AND swimming (more drowning risk). Temperature affects both variables.

✓ Why Random Assignment Helps

When you randomly assign people to groups, confounding factors—known and unknown—tend to be balanced across the groups. So if the groups end up different, the treatment is a plausible explanation. That's why a well-run randomized experiment can support a causal claim.

But design isn't everything. A causal conclusion also depends on how the experiment was carried out: Did people follow their assigned treatment? Did some drop out? Were outcomes measured the same way in both groups? Is the sample large enough?

✏️ Practice Problems

Problem 1: You randomly assign 50 students to group study and 50 to solo study. Group students average 82 points, solo students average 76. A two-sample t-test gives p = 0.04. What's the best conclusion?
We can't say anything about cause—this is an observational study
p = 0.04 proves that group study causes higher scores
Because students were randomly assigned, this supports a causal conclusion—provided the experiment was carried out well (no major dropout, scores measured fairly, etc.)
Correlation always implies causation
Problem 2: Across many cities and months, ice cream sales and crime rates have correlation r = 0.85. What's the most likely confounding variable?

Crime causes ice cream sales
Temperature/weather—hot weather increases both ice cream sales AND time spent outdoors

Part 3 · How hypotheses are tested
04 · Statistical Significance

What Does "Statistically Significant" Mean?

💡 Think in Stories: Basketball Free Throws

Your friend claims he makes 95% of his free throws. You watch him take 10 shots, and he makes only 3.

What do you think? "I don't believe that claim."

Why? Because making only 3 out of 10 would be very unusual for a 95% shooter. It's not impossible—he could be having a terrible day—but the evidence is against his claim.

That reasoning is exactly how a hypothesis test works.

Plain English Definition

A result is statistically significant when the data provide evidence against the null hypothesis, because the observed result would be unusual if that hypothesis were true.

"If nothing special were going on, a result like this would be surprising—so the data cast doubt on 'nothing special.'"

What it DOES mean

If H₀ (and the test's assumptions) were true, a result this extreme—or more extreme—would be unlikely. So the data provide evidence against H₀.

What it does NOT mean
  • It does not prove H₀ is false or that chance played no role.
  • It is not the probability that H₀ is true.
  • It doesn't mean the effect is large, important, or causal.

Example: Testing Michael's Coin

1

Set Up Hypotheses

H₀ (Null): The coin is fair (50% heads)
Hₐ (Alternative): The coin is NOT fair (too many or too few heads)

2

Collect Data

Flip the coin 25 times → observe 21 heads

3

How Unusual Is This?

If the coin were fair, we'd expect about 12–13 heads. 21 is far from that.

4

Calculate the P-Value

Simulate 1,000 sets of 25 fair-coin flips. Because Hₐ is "not fair," count results at least as far from 12.5 in either direction (21+ heads or 4 or fewer). Only about 1 in 1,000 is that extreme → p ≈ 0.001 (the exact binomial value is about 0.0009).

5

Decision

p ≈ 0.001 < α = 0.05 → Reject H₀. The data provide strong evidence that the coin is not fair. (We haven't proven it—a fair coin can produce 21 heads, just very rarely.)

💡 What Is a P-Value? (Plain English)

Formula way: The probability of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming H₀ (and the test's assumptions) are true.

Story way: "If nothing special were going on, how often would we see something this surprising—or more surprising?"

Small p-value → the result would be rare under H₀ → evidence against H₀. Large p-value → the result is consistent with H₀ (which is not the same as proving H₀ true).

✏️ Practice Problem

Problem 3: You suspect a coin favors tails. H₀: the coin is fair. Hₐ: it lands tails more than half the time. You flip it 30 times and get 22 tails. In 1,000 simulations of a fair coin, only 8 produced 22 or more tails. What's the p-value, and what do you conclude at α = 0.05?
p-value = 0.22; fail to reject H₀
p-value ≈ 0.008; reject H₀—the data provide evidence the coin favors tails
p-value ≈ 0.008; this proves the coin is biased
p-value = 0.73; reject H₀
Part 3 · How hypotheses are tested
05 · Key Terms

Hypothesis Testing Vocabulary

Term Formal Definition Story Definition
Null Hypothesis (H₀) The default claim being tested—usually "no effect" or "no difference" "The coin is fair" / "Sleep and GPA are unrelated"
Alternative Hypothesis (Hₐ) The claim we look for evidence for; can be one-sided (< or >) or two-sided (≠) "The coin is biased" / "More sleep goes with higher GPA"
Test Statistic A number summarizing how far the sample is from what H₀ predicts "21 heads in 25 flips" / "t = 2.95"
P-value P(result at least this extreme | H₀ true) "How surprising is my result if nothing special is going on?"
Significance Level (α) Cutoff chosen before looking at the data; also the Type I error rate you accept "How unusual is unusual enough?" (Commonly 0.05 = 5%)
✓ Making the Decision

If p ≤ α: Reject H₀. The result is statistically significant—the data provide evidence against H₀. (Not proof.)

If p > α: Fail to reject H₀. The data are consistent with H₀—but that does not prove H₀ is true. You may simply not have enough data to detect an effect.

Part 4 · What can go wrong
06 · Errors, Confounding & P-Hacking

No Test Is Perfect

Every hypothesis test makes a decision from incomplete information, so it can be wrong. There are two kinds of wrong decisions:

Type I Error (False Positive)

What it is: Rejecting H₀ when it's actually true

Plain English: You think something is happening when it's not

Example: You conclude sleep and GPA are related when they actually aren't

Analogy: Fire alarm goes off when there's no fire

How often? When H₀ is true, this happens with probability α (e.g., 5%).

Type II Error (False Negative)

What it is: Failing to reject H₀ when it's actually false

Plain English: You miss something that's actually happening

Example: You conclude sleep and GPA aren't related when they actually are

Analogy: Fire alarm stays silent when there IS a fire

How to reduce it: Larger samples give a test more power to detect real effects.

Confounding (Revisited)

A test can be calculated perfectly and still answer the wrong question. If the data are observational, a significant relationship may be driven by a confounding variable (Part 2). Statistical significance tells you a pattern is unlikely to be chance under H₀—it does not tell you why the pattern exists.

Significance ≠ Importance

With a very large sample, even a tiny, unimportant difference can produce a small p-value. Always look at the size of the effect (e.g., "GPA is 0.25 points higher"), not just whether p is below 0.05.

⚠️ Ethics: P-Hacking

P-hacking: Trying analyses until something crosses p < 0.05, then reporting only that. It inflates false positives and is unethical.

Examples:

  • Running many tests and only reporting the ones that "work" (with 20 tests at α = 0.05, about one false positive is expected even when nothing is going on)
  • Stopping data collection as soon as you hit p < 0.05
  • Excluding outliers or switching outcomes without a pre-stated reason

Defense: Decide your hypotheses, metric, sample size, and α before collecting data, and report everything you tested.

✏️ Practice Problem

Problem 4: You're testing a focus app. H₀: the app has no effect. You reject H₀, but in reality the app doesn't work. What type of error is this?
Type I Error (False Positive)—you think it works when it doesn't
Type II Error (False Negative)—you miss a real effect
No error—you made the right decision

Part 5 · How inference is applied
07 · Types of Inference

Matching the Test to the Question

The right test depends on what kind of variable you measured and what you're comparing:

Question Data type Typical test
Is a proportion different from a benchmark? Categorical (yes/no) One-proportion z-test, exact binomial test, or simulation
Is a mean different from a benchmark? Numerical One-sample t-test
Do two groups differ in their proportions? Categorical (yes/no) Two-proportion z-test or randomization test
Do two groups differ in their means? Numerical Two-sample t-test (or randomization test)
Are two numerical variables related? Two numerical Test for correlation

1. Inference for a Proportion Hypothetical data

When to use: Studying what fraction of a population has a characteristic.

Example question: "Is the proportion of students at Hazel & Everett's school who get 8+ hours of sleep below 0.5?"

Hypotheses

H₀: p = 0.5
Hₐ: p < 0.5 (one-sided)

Data

Random sample of n = 64 students: 21 get 8+ hours → p̂ = 21/64 ≈ 0.328

Simulation: 1,000 samples of 64 students, assuming H₀ (p = 0.5) is true
Running simulation…
📊 Reading the Chart

If H₀ were true, sample proportions would cluster around 0.5 (the peak). Our observed 0.328 sits far out in the left tail.

p-value ≈ 0.004 (exact binomial). Your simulation gives a similar estimate—it will vary slightly each run, because simulations are random.

Interpretation: If half of all students got 8+ hours of sleep, a sample proportion this low or lower would be very unusual. The data provide strong evidence that the proportion at this school is below 0.5.

✏️ Practice Problem

Problem 5: A random sample of 80 PCCC students finds that 31 (about 39%) get 8+ hours of sleep. H₀: p = 0.5; Hₐ: p < 0.5. The p-value is about 0.03. What does this mean in plain English?
PCCC students sleep more than the benchmark
If the true proportion were 50%, a sample result this low or lower would happen only about 3% of the time. This is evidence (at α = 0.05) that the PCCC proportion is below 50%.
There is a 3% chance that H₀ is true
The result is not statistically significant

2. Inference for a Mean (t-tests) Hypothetical data

When to use: Studying the average of a numerical variable.

Example question: "Is the average GPA at Hazel & Everett's school higher than 3.0?"

One-Sample t-test

Compares one sample mean to a benchmark value (e.g., "Is our mean GPA above 3.0?")

Two-Sample t-test

Compares the means of two groups (e.g., "Do group-study students score higher than solo-study students?")

📚 Hazel & Everett's GPA Data (Hypothetical)

H₀: μ = 3.0    Hₐ: μ > 3.0 (μ = mean GPA of all students at the school)

Sample: Random sample of n = 50 students. Sample mean x̄ = 3.25, sample standard deviation s = 0.60.

Calculation: Standard error = s / √n = 0.60 / √50 ≈ 0.085
t = (x̄ − 3.0) / SE = 0.25 / 0.085 ≈ 2.95, with 49 degrees of freedom → one-sided p ≈ 0.002

Assumptions to check: the students are a random sample, responses are independent, and GPAs aren't so skewed that a sample of 50 is unreliable (GPA is capped at 4.0, so look at a histogram).

Plain English: If the school's true mean GPA were 3.0, a sample mean of 3.25 or higher would be unusual (about 2 in 1,000 samples). The data provide evidence that the school's mean GPA is above 3.0—by roughly a quarter of a grade point in this sample.

# In Python (with the 50 GPAs stored in a list called gpas)
from scipy import stats
result = stats.ttest_1samp(gpas, popmean=3.0, alternative='greater')
print(result.statistic, result.pvalue)

3. Inference for a Correlation Hypothetical data

When to use: Studying whether two numerical variables have a linear relationship.

Example question: "Is there a positive relationship between hours of sleep and GPA?"

Correlation (r)

r = +1: Perfect positive linear relationship
r = 0: No linear relationship
r = −1: Perfect negative linear relationship

Hazel & Everett

H₀: ρ = 0 (no linear relationship in the population)
Hₐ: ρ > 0
Data: n = 50 students, sample r = 0.47 → t ≈ 3.69, one-sided p ≈ 0.0003

⚠️ Remember: Correlation ≠ Causation

A significant r = 0.47 is evidence of an association between sleep and GPA. It does NOT show that sleep causes higher GPAs. This is observational data—confounders such as work hours, course load, or health could affect both.

To support a causal claim, you'd need a well-designed and well-run randomized experiment.

Part 5 · How inference is applied
08 · A/B Testing

Randomized Experiments in Industry

Companies apply everything above through A/B testing: a randomized experiment that compares two versions of a product, page, or message.

1

Plan Before You Launch

Choose the main metric, the hypotheses, α, and the sample size in advance (this is your defense against p-hacking).

2

Create Two Versions

Version A (control) and Version B (treatment with one change)

3

Randomly Assign Users

Each user is randomly shown A or B, so the groups are comparable.

4

Measure Outcomes

Track the planned metric—clicks, purchases, sign-ups, revenue.

5

Analyze with the Right Test

Rates (proportions like "% who clicked") → two-proportion z-test or randomization test. Averages of numerical outcomes (like dollars spent) → two-sample t-test, if its assumptions are reasonable.

6

Decide and Monitor

Weigh statistical evidence and practical size of the effect, then roll out—and keep watching the metric afterward.

Example: Streaming Service Ratings Hypothetical

A fictional streaming service wants more viewers to rate what they watch. It randomly assigns 10,000 viewers to one of two rating designs. The metric is the proportion of viewers who leave a rating.

Version A · Control

Star Ratings (1–5 stars)

5,000 viewers → 300 left a rating (6.0%)

Version B · Treatment

Thumbs Up / Down

5,000 viewers → 380 left a rating (7.6%)

Because the outcome is yes/no, the right tool is a two-proportion z-test: z ≈ 3.18, two-sided p ≈ 0.0015. Viewers shown thumbs were more likely to rate. Because assignment was random, it's reasonable to attribute the difference to the design—though the company should also ask whether a thumbs rating is as useful as a star rating for its recommendations.

Example: Nonprofit Donation Button Hypothetical

A nonprofit randomly shows each website visitor one of two donation buttons. It tracks two different metrics, and each needs its own test:

Version Visitors Donations Conversion Rate Avg Gift (donors only)
A: "Find a Borrower" 3,000 120 4.0% $25.00 (SD $12)
B: "Invest in Hope" 3,000 165 5.5% $25.42 (SD $12)
Metric 1 · Conversion Rate (proportion)

Test: Two-proportion z-test (or randomization test)

Result: 5.5% vs. 4.0% → z ≈ 2.73, two-sided p ≈ 0.006

Plain English: If both buttons had the same true donation rate, a gap this large would be unusual. Evidence that B gets a higher share of visitors to donate.

Metric 2 · Average Gift (numerical)

Test: Two-sample (Welch) t-test—reasonable here because each group has 100+ donors

Result: $25.42 vs. $25.00 → t ≈ 0.29, p ≈ 0.77

Plain English: No evidence that the button changes how much people give once they decide to donate.

✓ Decision

Version B appears to increase how many visitors donate, with no evidence that it changes how much each donor gives. Rolling out B is reasonable—and the nonprofit should keep monitoring donations afterward.

Lesson: Match the test to the metric. Counts and rates are proportions; dollar amounts are numerical measurements.

09 · Summary

Key Takeaways

1 · Why Inference

Sample → Population

Use sample data to draw conclusions about a larger population, with honest uncertainty.

2 · Evidence

Design Matters

Observational studies show associations; well-run randomized experiments can support causal claims.

3 · Testing

p ≤ α

The result would be unusual if H₀ were true → evidence against H₀ (not proof).

4 · Pitfalls

Errors & Bias

Type I/II errors, confounding, and p-hacking can all lead to wrong conclusions.

5 · Applied

Match the Test

Proportions, means, and correlations each need their own test—including in A/B tests.

Remember: Think in stories, not formulas. A significant result means "this would be surprising if nothing special were going on"—it is evidence, not proof. Correlation ≠ causation (watch for confounders). A p-value alone never establishes causation; the study design and how it was carried out matter too.
